<h3>Why are we publishing this?</h3>
<p>Because the renter complaint we kept finding online isn't about rent. It's this one, typed on Quora by a tenant in Delhi:</p>
<blockquote>"I am living in Delhi and my landlord is not returning my security deposit money. His son is a lawyer. What can I do?"</blockquote>
<p>Almost all of that is decided on the day you sign, at a stamp paper vendor, in about ten minutes. So here's what to read before you sign. No sales pitch. If you want the full checklist as a printable page, there's a line at the end.</p>
</div>

<h2><span class="n">1.</span>"How much deposit is legal?"</h2>
<p>There's no national cap. In Bengaluru, 6 to 10 months' rent is custom, not law; Karnataka has no legal limit. In Mumbai and Pune, 3 to 6 months is common. Only the states that adopted the Model Tenancy Act (Tamil Nadu, Uttar Pradesh, Assam, Andhra Pradesh and some union territories) cap a residential deposit at 2 months' rent.</p>
<p>A big deposit is legal. It's also negotiable. Ask for less, or ask for interest on it. The worst answer you'll get is no.</p>

<h2><span class="n">2.</span>The clause that can cost you all of it</h2>
<p>Look for a line like "if the tenant vacates before the lock-in period, the entire security deposit shall be forfeited". It reads like a rule. It's closer to a bluff.</p>
<p>Under section 74 of the Indian Contract Act, a court won't enforce a penalty, only reasonable compensation for the loss actually suffered. The Supreme Court applied exactly that to a forfeited deposit in <em>Maula Bux v. Union of India</em> (1969). For a flat, the real loss is usually the rent for the rest of the lock-in or the notice period, minus whatever the landlord earns by letting it to someone else.</p>
<p>So ask to replace it with this:</p>
<blockquote>"If the tenant leaves before the lock-in ends, the tenant will pay rent for the remaining lock-in period or one month's rent, whichever is lower, which may be adjusted from the deposit."</blockquote>
<p>And make the lock-in mutual. If you can't leave for six months, neither can they ask you to.</p>

<h2><span class="n">3.</span>"Painting and cleaning charges will be deducted"</h2>
<p>A deposit covers three things: unpaid rent, unpaid bills, and damage beyond normal wear and tear. Faded paint, small scuffs and fittings that aged with use are normal wear and tear. A clause that deducts painting "whatever the condition" moves the owner's upkeep bill onto you.</p>
<p>Ask to limit it to damage beyond normal wear and tear, or to a fixed amount written into the agreement. A number you can see beats a bill you can't.</p>

<h2><span class="n">4.</span>"When does the landlord have to return the deposit?"</h2>
<p>Most agreements don't say, and that silence is where months of waiting come from. Write a number in: the deposit comes back within, say, 15 days of handing over the keys, with an itemised list (and bills) for anything deducted. Anywhere from 7 to 30 days is reasonable.</p>

<h2><span class="n">5.</span>Why it's always 11 months, and what "notarised" doesn't do</h2>
<p>Under section 17 of the Registration Act, 1908, a lease for more than a year has to be registered. So most agreements run 11 months, on stamp paper, often with a notary's stamp.</p>
<p>That's valid between you and the landlord in most states. But a notary only confirms who signed. It isn't registration. If your deposit is large, registering is cheap insurance; many states let you do it online.</p>

<h2><span class="n">6.</span>In Maharashtra, it has to be registered</h2>
<p>Section 55 of the Maharashtra Rent Control Act, 1999 says every leave and licence agreement must be in writing and registered, whatever its length, and it puts that duty on the landlord. Registration runs online through the state's IGR portal. If you're renting in Mumbai or Pune and nobody mentions registration, ask.</p>

<h2><span class="n">7.</span>"Landlord may revise the rent at any time"</h2>
<p>Delete it. A fixed increase at renewal (5% to 10%) is normal. An open power to raise rent whenever the owner likes defeats the point of signing a term at all.</p>
<p>And do the maths on the normal kind. On ₹30,000, 10% every 11 months is about ₹36,300 after two renewals.</p>

<h2><span class="n">8.</span>"Landlord may inspect the premises at any time"</h2>
<p>Ask for at least 24 hours' written notice, at a reasonable hour, with immediate entry only for real emergencies (fire, flood, a gas leak). Model Tenancy Act states already require that notice; everywhere else, write it in.</p>

<h2><span class="n">9.</span>Who fixes what</h2>
<p>Structural problems, major plumbing and electrical faults: the owner. Bulbs and tap washers: you. Write it down, with a time limit for the big ones. "Landlord will repair within 7 days of written notice" saves a month of follow-up messages.</p>

<h2><span class="n">10.</span>The 15 minutes that win deposit fights</h2>
<p>On the day you move in, take a video of every room: walls, floors, switches, taps, the inside of cupboards. Make an inventory list (fan, geyser, curtains, keys) and get the landlord to sign it or reply "ok" on WhatsApp. Do the same on the day you leave.</p>
<p>Keep the agreement, proof of every deposit and rent payment, and the key handover message. That folder is your whole case if it comes to one.</p>

<h2><span class="n">11.</span>"The landlord won't return my deposit. What can I do?"</h2>
<ol class="tight">
<li>Ask in writing (email or WhatsApp, so there's a record) for the full deposit or an itemised list with bills, and give a date, say 15 days.</li>
<li>No answer? A lawyer's legal notice costs a few thousand rupees, and many landlords pay at this stage.</li>
<li>Then file: the Rent Authority or Rent Court in Model Tenancy Act states, the Small Causes Court in Mumbai, and a civil suit for recovery of money elsewhere. Consumer commissions generally don't hear landlord and tenant deposit disputes.</li>
</ol>
